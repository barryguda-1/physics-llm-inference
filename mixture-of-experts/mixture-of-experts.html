Section 1 · Interactive

01Dense vs MoE: replace the FFN with a committee

A standard transformer runs every token through the same FFN — for a 7B model, w1: 4096×11008 and w2: 11008×4096. Mixture of Experts replaces that single FFN with many smaller expert FFNs plus a router that decides, per token, which experts to use. The book's routing example, live: token 1 → experts 2 & 5, token 2 → 1 & 5, token 3 → 2 & 7. Click a token to trace it through both halves.

Trade: MoE buys more total capacity with less active compute per token — the cost is complexity in routing and expert load balancing (§5). Key parameters from the book: num_experts typically 8–64, top_k typically 1–2, per-expert d_ff smaller than the dense FFN. Qwen3-30B-A3B: 8 experts, top_k=2. Mixtral, Qwen-MoE and DeepSeek get their better quality per FLOP from exactly this sparsity — the same inference principles apply, only the memory-access pattern of the FFN changes.

Section 2 · Interactive

02The router: 32K parameters that decide everything

The router is the smallest layer in the model — a single linear projection 4096 → 8 producing one logit per expert. Then: top-k picks the winners, a softmax over just those k logits turns them into mixing coefficients, and the token's FFN output is the weighted sum. Drop tokens into the space — by hand or as a stream — and watch regions of expert specialization emerge: exactly the skew that expert caching (§4) feeds on.

click anywhere to route a token · colors = expert regions

router logits = x @ W_r · Linear(4096→8) · 32K params

route a token to see its logits, top-k picks and mixing weights

Book mechanics, exactly: weights, indices = torch.topk(router_logits, top_k) then weights = F.softmax(weights, dim=-1) — the softmax runs over the k selected logits only, so the coefficients always sum to 1. If expert 3 scored 0.7 and expert 5 scored 0.3, the layer outputs 0.7·expert_3(x) + 0.3·expert_5(x). A well-trained router specializes its experts, creating predictable — and skewed — access patterns: that is why some experts are hot and others rarely used (§4), and why an unbalanced router wastes the fleet (§5). Every expert still contributes only a small FFN: capacity lives in the count of experts, not their size.

Section 3 · Interactive

03Sort, then group: the scattered-batch fix

In a dense model every token does the same computation — perfect for big batched GEMMs. In MoE, tokens scatter: token 1 needs experts 2 & 5, token 2 needs 1 & 5… The naive loop-over-experts runs one tiny GEMM per expert over scattered tokens — small batches, many launches, poor utilization. The fix that makes MoE nearly as efficient as dense at large batch: flatten the token–expert pairs, sort by expert, find each expert's contiguous range (searchsorted), and hand it one batched GEMM chunk. Press play and watch the permutation.

Efficiency is a function of batch size — the book's three lines, verbatim: batch 1 → 0.25 tokens/expert avg — very small GEMMs, poor efficiency; batch 64 → 16 tokens/expert — decent GEMM sizes, reasonable efficiency; batch 256 → 64 tokens/expert — good GEMM sizes, high efficiency — for large batches, grouping makes MoE nearly as efficient as dense. The sort itself is cheap (indices.flatten().sort() over batch × top_k small ints) and the permutation is one gather. A grouped-GEMM kernel (or expert-parallel kernel) then walks the contiguous ranges back-to-back — the same m-size as the naive version's biggest case, but with zero gather/scatter per expert and no launch-per-expert on the critical path. Display shows the first 48 tokens of the batch; meters use the full batch.

04The expert cache: 60 GB of weights, 4 slots of VRAM

Qwen3-30B-A3B's 30B parameters are 60 GB at fp16 — most of a single 80 GB H100 before any KV cache; the B200's 192 GB is headroom. When experts don't all fit, treat GPU memory as a cache: keep the most recently used experts resident and stream the rest in from CPU memory or disk. Skewed routing (§2) is what makes this viable — flip the router distribution and watch the cache thrash. Prefetching on a second CUDA stream hides what latency it can.

The book's LRU, faithfully: on a hit, the expert moves to most-recently-used; on a miss with a full cache, evict the LRU expert and load the new one from CPU memory or disk. Model: step compute ≈ 10 ms; an expert load ≈ 30 ms over PCIe, so an unprefetched miss costs a 40 ms step; a predicted miss loads on a second CUDA stream while the current batch computes — the load overlaps the 10 ms and only 30 ms of it remains exposed (30 ms of every miss still beats nothing: transfers outlast one layer's compute, so prefetch reduces, not removes, stall). The predictor reads the next layer's router logits — the book's PrefetchingExpertCache. Why skew saves you: with 8 experts and cache size 4, a well-trained router's popularity ranking keeps the same ~4 experts hot — the top-4 experts here carry ~94% of assignments, so the hit rate settles near 90% — while a uniform router misses every expert it hasn't already cached (~50%, thrashing).

Section 5 · Interactive

05Load balance: six idle experts are a 2× bill

Nothing forces a trained router to spread tokens evenly. Slide the skew toward "everyone picks the same two" and watch six experts sit idle while two do everything — the book's verdict: total compute is 2× what it should be, because we cannot parallelize across experts. The production damper is an expert capacity: each expert takes at most C tokens; overflow tokens are rerouted to less busy experts — trading routing quality for efficiency. Training adds auxiliary losses to encourage balance; at inference we live with whatever routing the trained model produces.

The book's load_balanced_routing: count each expert's assignments; when a pick would exceed expert_capacity, reroute to the first expert under capacity (the sim uses least-loaded — same idea, smoother). Full skew is the book's disaster case: expert 0: 100% load · expert 1: 100% load · experts 2–7: 0% — total compute 2× what it should be. Capacity is the knob that trades quality (suboptimal routing) for efficiency (balanced load): set it to the balanced share (8) and watch the hot pair's overflow spread across the idle six — every rerouted tick is a token that got a slightly wrong expert so the GPU stayed full. 32 tokens × top-2 = 64 assignments over 8 experts.

Section 6 · October 2026

06Since the book: MoE ate the frontier

The book teaches MoE through an 8-expert model — that design choice won. Six things that shipped after the book's examples, each tied back to the section it grew out of.

Dec 2024 → 2025

Fine-grained + shared experts: DeepSeek-V3 / R1

671B total, 37B active: 256 routed experts per layer plus 1 shared expert every token always uses, top-8 routing. Fine-grained (many small) experts give more combinations per FLOP; the shared expert carries the load common to all tokens. And the §5 problem got a training-time answer: auxiliary-loss-free balancing via per-expert bias terms — balance without the usual auxiliary-loss quality tax.

← grows §5: load balancing moved from inference patch to architecture
Aug 2025

OpenAI rejoined open weights — with MoE

gpt-oss-120b: 117B total, 5.1B active, 128 routed experts + 1 shared, top-4 — and the §1 memory row solved by quantization: MoE weights in MXFP4 so the whole model fits a single 80 GB GPU (H100 / MI300X). Its sibling gpt-oss-20b: 21B total, 3.6B active, 32 experts — runs in 16 GB, i.e. on a laptop. Apache 2.0.

← grows §1 + §4: 60 GB of fp16 was only ever a rendering of this row
Jul 2025

Giant scale: Kimi K2

Moonshot's Kimi K2: 1T total parameters, 32B active — the book's "A3B" naming stretched to A32B — with 384 experts + 1 shared, top-8, 128K context, open weights (modified MIT). The pattern held all the way up: capacity grows with expert count, active compute stays flat, and serving it is an expert-parallelism problem more than a FLOP problem.

← grows §1: capacity without compute, at 33× the book's example
Feb 2025 → 2026

Expert parallelism got its own library: DeepEP

DeepSeek open-sourced DeepEP: the all-to-all dispatch / combine kernels MoE serving needs when experts are sharded across GPUs — NVLink inside the node, RDMA across nodes, plus a low-latency mode for decode with hooks that overlap communication and computation. vLLM and SGLang expose expert parallelism (--ep-size) on top of this layer; routing became a network operation.

← grows §3: the sort-and-group trick, extended across GPUs
2025 → 2026

Grouped GEMM grew up

The §3 sort→contiguous-chunks idea is now every engine's default path, in silicon-shaped form: DeepSeek's DeepGEMM (JIT-compiled grouped GEMMs for MoE, contiguous and masked layouts), CUTLASS fused MoE GEMMs in vLLM, grouped GEMM in FlashInfer (Chapter 6's library), and Triton fused_moe. One kernel launch per layer instead of a launch per expert — the book's "solution is grouped GEMM" line, productized.

← grows §3: contiguous chunks became first-class kernel shapes
Apr 2025 → 2026

The book's example model got a family

Qwen3-30B-A3B gained the Qwen3-235B-A22B flagship — 235B total, 22B active, 128 experts, top-8 — plus MoE and dense siblings at every scale, and update releases since. The frontier-open default is now MoE: Mixtral (the book's other mention) was simply early. Serving stacks treat "how many experts fit, where, and how do we all-to-all them" as a first-class scheduling question — Chapter 10 territory.
